## The attack that nearly worked

The interesting row is the slow deliberate shake: a hand moving the phone
rhythmically at walking cadence, which is what a motivated person actually
tries. It matches walking on frequency, on skew, on orientation drift, and
even on step variance.

It fails on one thing. A walking body rotates as it steps, so gyroscope
pitch rate correlates with vertical acceleration. That reads **0.997 for a
real walk and 0.060 for the shake** — the feature separates them perfectly.

At equal weighting the attack still scored **0.689 and credited**, because
one failing signal out of six could not outvote five passing ones. The fix
was not a new feature; it was weighting the decisive one to 3.6 against
roughly 1.0 for the others:

| Weight on gyro coupling | Best attack score | Headroom below 0.62 |
|---|---|---|
| 1.4 | 0.689 | **−0.069 — credits** |
| 2.0 | 0.617 | 0.003 |
| 2.4 | 0.579 | 0.041 |
| 3.0 | 0.532 | 0.088 |
| **3.6** | **0.494** | **0.126** |
| 4.5 | 0.449 | 0.171 |

Every modelled walk stays above 0.99 across that whole range, so the weight
costs nothing on the positive side. 3.6 was chosen because returns flatten
after it and leaning harder on a single signal is imprudent.

## Two gates ahead of the features

**Motion floor.** The six features are shape statistics, and the shape of
sensor noise lands inside the walking bands by accident — a motionless
phone scores 0.26 on features alone. Below a spread of 0.02g, confidence is
zero regardless.

**Geometric combination.** Scores are combined as a weighted geometric mean,
not an average, so the result collapses when any signature is absent rather
than being outvoted. Each term is floored at 0.05 so one glitched sample
degrades the score steeply instead of reading as fraud.

## What this does and does not establish

**Does:** that the decision boundary has the right shape, that each feature
carries the discriminating power claimed for it, and that the specific
attacks modelled here are rejected with a margin. It also catches
regressions — this table is a test, and it fails the build if an attack
starts crediting.

**Does not:** that the thresholds are correct for a given handset and a
given person. These are models, not recordings. The separation is real; the
exact numbers need calibration against logged sessions from several people,
several carry positions and several devices.

One honest observation from building this: the hardest positive case is the
one we could not synthesise. Pearson correlation is scale-invariant, so a
coherent rotation signal survives heavy noise, and every model of a
"difficult" walk still scored above 0.99. Whether a real gait exists that
genuinely lacks the coupling — a very stiff carry, a prosthetic, a wheeled
aid — is a question only recorded data can answer, and it is the first
thing calibration should look for.
